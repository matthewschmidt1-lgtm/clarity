The problem is rarely information

The world gives you more information. Clarity helps you make sense of it.

Noise is too many opinions, endless searching, one loud fear, false certainty, and the feeling of going around in circles.

Most people stuck on a decision don't need more facts. They have one of these problems:

  • They haven't separated facts from assumptions.
  • They're treating an uncertain outcome as certain.
  • They're comparing options over different time horizons.
  • They're afraid of one outcome and ignoring the rest.
  • They already know the answer but haven't named the tradeoff.

A different kind of intelligence

Clarity asks before it advises.

You don't need another opinion. You need to see the decision clearly.

We'll separate what you know from what you think you know, identify what matters, and show you what could change the picture. Most AI tries to appear intelligent. Clarity is built to make you feel intelligent.

The method

Know. Weigh. See.

Ten quiet questions. Sophisticated underneath, simple on top. The conclusion is held back until you've explored the decision, because the exploration is where the clarity comes from.

Then: make your call. The decision remains yours.

01 · Know

What is true, what is assumed, what is unknown?

Every piece of information you share is sorted. A fact is something you could verify today. A belief is something you're treating as true. An unknown is something that could materially change the picture.

  • "The new job pays $110,000." Fact.
  • "I'll get promoted within two years." Belief.
  • "How many hours I'll actually work." Unknown.
02 · Weigh

What matters, how much, and what are you willing to risk?

Clarity separates six months from five years, action-regret from inaction-regret, expected outcome from worst plausible outcome, and asks the question most tools skip: if you dislike it, how easy is it to undo?

  • Short-term comfort vs. long-term opportunity
  • Reversibility and the options a choice opens or closes
  • The minimum outcome you're genuinely willing to accept
03 · See

What changes the decision, and what should you learn next?

Before anything is calculated, you predict. Then Clarity argues the other side, tests how sensitive your call is to each assumption, and finds the single unanswered question that could most change it.

  • Your estimate vs. a defensible range, and what explains the gap
  • The strongest case for the option you're not leaning toward
  • "Don't decide yet" is always on the table, with its real cost

A language for thinking

The Clarity Lens

After fifteen minutes of talking it through, Clarity says: here's what you've told me. Here's what you know. Here's what you believe. Here's what you don't know. And here's the assumption your decision depends on most.

A small vocabulary you'll start using on your own. That's the idea.

Known

What you actually know. Things you could verify today.

Assumed

What you're treating as true. Often the whole decision rests here.

Unknown

What could materially change the decision if you found it out.

The Tradeoff

What you're giving up to get something else. Named plainly.

The Threshold

The worst outcome you're genuinely willing to accept.

The Next Question

The one piece of information most worth finding out before you decide.

The Pivot

Your decision changes depending on one thing: how confident you are that the new job improves your quality of life.

Instead of twenty factors, Clarity identifies the one that actually matters, and what it would take to know it.

The payoff

Not an answer. A report.

Clarity never says "take the job." It shows you what you're actually deciding, what you know, what you're assuming, what could happen, how much each outcome matters to you, what the answer is sensitive to, and the one thing you could learn that would most improve your decision.

The decision remains yours. That isn't a disclaimer. It's the product.

Intellectual honesty

We don't know yet.

In a world where software answers everything confidently, restraint is the differentiator. These rules are not features. They're the brand.

  1. 01Never manufacture precision.
  2. 02Never hide uncertainty.
  3. 03Never confuse correlation with causation.
  4. 04Never pretend preferences are facts.
  5. 05Never make the decision for you.
  6. 06Never bury the assumption driving the result.
  7. 07Never ask a question unless the answer could matter.

Over time

It learns how you reason about your own future.

Six months ago you estimated an 80% chance that changing jobs would make you happier. What actually happened? Answer that a few times and Clarity starts to see your patterns: where you're optimistic, where you're cautious, where you're right.

Not because it knows the future. Because it learns how you predict it.
