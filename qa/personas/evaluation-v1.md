# Clarity — persona evaluation

Eight people ran the seven questions through the real engine (`qa/replay.sh` against `qa/personas/sessions.json`; full transcripts and structured models in `qa/personas/transcripts.txt`). Below, each persona speaks for themselves about the report they got, then a scorecard, then cross-cutting findings.

---

## job-change — Priya

**1. Before / after.** Before: I picked this fight with myself weeks ago and I'm tired of circling it — half of me wants the excitement, half of me thinks I'm bored, not stuck. After: it named the exact thing I hadn't said out loud, that I'm assuming the startup means growth without any evidence. **Lighter.**

**2. Did anything become clearer?** "I know what I need to find out." The report's actual next step — "Is 'Take the new job at the startup' really stronger on career growth?" — is a thing I can go verify (ask people who left, ask about the actual scope of the role), not a thing I have to feel my way through.

**3. Different from asking a friend / Googling / ChatGPT?** A friend would ask "well, do you like the people?" and a pros-and-cons list would just tally the four factors and call it a tie. This didn't tally — it picked one factor and said that one is the whole thing. That's the part a list can't do.

**4. Salience.** Maybe. If I'm this stuck again I'd remember "find the one thing," but I don't think I'd remember the name Clarity unless the ritual (the falling-leaf pause) sticks in memory too.

**5. Skeptical read.** "Assumed: 'Take the new job at the startup' is stronger on career growth. 'Stay where I am' is stronger on free time." — reading it back, "stronger on free time" makes staying sound like a settled fact, when really I just guessed the startup would eat my evenings. It's presented with the same flat confidence as the things I actually verified.

**6. The Pivot.** Yes — whether the startup is actually more growth, or just louder about it, is the real fork. I would not have named "day-to-day environment" (the other unknown) as the crux; growth is right.

## move-home — Daniel

**1. Before / after.** Before: guilt-paralyzed, treating "move" as the only responsible answer. After: relieved that it separated the guilt from the actual bet — "Can 'Stay here' actually give you to not feel guilty every time your phone rings?" — because that's not really about geography at all. **Lighter.**

**2. Did anything become clearer?** "I know what I need to find out." Whether staying and putting a real support plan in place (calls, visits, a local check-in person) could kill the guilt without uprooting my life.

**3. Different?** Completely different from what a friend would say ("of course you should move, it's your mom") or what Googling "should I move to be near aging parents" returns (guilt-driven listicles). It refused to treat the guilt as the answer.

**4. Salience.** Yes. The reframe — "you may not be deciding whether to move... you may be deciding whether you can get what you need without it" — is the kind of sentence I'd actually remember at 2am.

**5. Skeptical read.** "You don't just want to move back home to be near my parents." — that's a leftover from my own phrasing of the option label; it reads like the app is talking about *its own* parents. Mechanical, not what I said in that form.

**6. The Pivot.** Mostly yes, though the report offers two things at once — the hope-check ("can staying give you that") and, as a fallback, "which option is actually stronger on relationships" — and doesn't make clear which one to actually go test first if the first answer is inconclusive.

## grad-school — Marisol

**1. Before / after.** Before: torn between a purpose I want and a stability I'm scared to give up, and annoyed that everyone keeps telling me PhDs are "just five years." After: the tradeoff line named the real trade cleanly. **Lighter.**

**2. Did anything become clearer?** "I know what I need to find out" — specifically whether the PhD really moves my career forward or is a five-year detour, since that's the one thing I marked "don't know."

**3. Different?** Yes — a pros-and-cons list would list "less money" and "more meaning" as two separate line items of equal weight. This said explicitly: meaning and income are both known and pulling opposite ways, so the tiebreaker is the one thing that's actually uncertain (career growth), not a vote count.

**4. Salience.** Maybe — useful, but I'd want to see it handle "five years is a long time" as a factor of its own (time horizon), not just folded into "hard."

**5. Skeptical read.** "THE PIVOT: Can 'Stay in my job and look for a raise elsewhere' actually give you to finally be doing the kind of research you actually care about instead of assisting someone else's?" — "give you to finally be doing" reads like broken grammar, not something a person would ever say back to me. It undercuts the confidence of the moment that's supposed to be the payoff line.

**6. The Pivot.** Half right. The hope-check (can my current job ever give me real research work) is a fair thing to check, but I'd have said the actual crux is whether the *market* has a job for me at the end — which the report only surfaces as the secondary "career growth" question, buried in a sentence, not foregrounded.

## heavy-hearted-relationship — Aisha

**1. Before / after.** Before: crying on both sides of this, no reason I could point to. After: steadier, because it didn't try to manufacture a reason — it said the real fork is whether I even know what I want (meaning), not whether he did something wrong. **Lighter.**

**2. Did anything become clearer?** "I understand what I'm actually struggling with," more than "what to find out." The report is honest that this doesn't resolve into a research task the way a job or a house does.

**3. Different?** Very different from "ask a friend," who would ask me to list what's wrong with him — this never asked me to build a case against him. "On everything you named, 'Stay' is stronger" is disarming because it's true and I hadn't put it together.

**4. Salience.** Yes, specifically because of that line — it's the sentence I'd repeat to a therapist.

**5. Skeptical read.** "The only way to know is to try." — this is technically the "findOut: doing" branch working as designed, but for a six-year relationship it feels glib, like the app doesn't register the stakes of "trying" here (you can't test-leave a relationship the way you test a new job). It's the same line the engine would give someone testing a new gym.

**6. The Pivot.** Yes — meaning is the real crux, more than stability (which I already know favors staying) or relationships (an assumption I haven't actually tested). I would have named the same thing.

## buy-vs-rent — Ken

**1. Before / after.** Before: anxious that we're "throwing money away," parroting other people's certainty about renting being a mistake. After: calmer, because the report separated the actual unknown (can renting even solve the money-security feeling) from the settled stuff (we do know renting gives us more flexibility). **Lighter.**

**2. Did anything become clearer?** "I know what I need to find out" — specifically, whether the financial-security math actually favors buying in our market right now, which we've never actually run.

**3. Different?** Yes — every "buy vs rent" calculator online just spits out a break-even year. This didn't do math; it asked whether the *feeling* driving the question ("we're behind") is even fixable by buying, which no calculator asks.

**4. Salience.** Yes, mostly because of "The tradeoff isn't between the options; it's between what you're sure of and what you're not" style thinking — that reframes the FOMO-driven "everyone says" chatter usefully. (That specific line didn't fire for us since we had a two-sided tradeoff, but the tradeoff line that did fire, "More stability against more freedom and control," did the same job.)

**5. Skeptical read.** "Can 'Keep renting' actually give you to stop feeling like we're behind everyone else our age?" — again the "give you to stop feeling" construction. It's not something I'd ever say, and it makes the most important line in the whole report sound like a translation error.

**6. The Pivot.** Mostly — whether renting can kill the "behind everyone" feeling is fair to ask, but the report doesn't flag that "financial security: don't know" is itself resolvable with an afternoon of real math (rent-vs-buy calculators, actual mortgage rates), which feels like a missed chance to make "find out" concrete.

## start-business — Ola

**1. Before / after.** Before: guilty about even wanting this, treating "responsible" and "quit" as opposites. After: relieved the report didn't flinch from naming that staying is currently ahead on the things I know for sure. **About the same** — useful, but it didn't reduce the fear, just organized it.

**2. Did anything become clearer?** "I know what I need to find out" — specifically whether the business idea actually has room to grow (career growth marked unknown), which is more concrete than "is this the right time," the question I walked in with.

**3. Different?** Yes — the tradeoff line, "More meaning against more financial security," is a cleaner statement of the actual bet than any pros-and-cons list I've made, which always sprawls into a dozen bullet points that cancel each other out.

**5. Skeptical read.** "Can 'Stay' actually give you to build something that's actually mine instead of making someone else rich?" — "something that's actually mine" doesn't get converted to "yours" the way the rest of the sentence gets converted to second person, so it reads like the report is talking about the app's own possession, not mine. Small, but it's the kind of detail that makes the biggest line in the report feel machine-generated right when it should feel most personal.

**6. The Pivot.** I'd argue no, not quite — the report frames the pivot as "can staying give me the meaning of building something mine," but for three kids and a mortgage, the thing that actually turns this decision is runway (how many months we can survive if the business is slow to make money), which was never on my list of factors and so the engine can't surface it. That's a fidelity limitation, not a wrong pivot given what I told it — but I notice it.

**4. Salience.** Maybe — the tradeoff sentence would come back to me, but the pivot question wouldn't, because it doesn't match what I'd actually go verify.

## already-knows — Tom

**1. Before / after.** Before: not conflicted about the decision, just scared to say it out loud after months of running numbers. After: exactly named — "There's no unknown left that would materially change this decision. What's left is preference, and that's yours." **Lighter**, specifically relieved of the obligation to keep "being thorough."

**2. Did anything become clearer?** "I know what I need to do." This is the one persona where that's the honest answer, and the report says so plainly instead of manufacturing a false unknown to send me off to research.

**3. Different?** Yes, and this is the case where it matters most — a friend or ChatGPT would likely still offer a "have you considered..." even when there's nothing left to consider, because open-ended chat tools default to being generative. This one had the discipline to say "nothing" and stop.

**4. Salience.** Yes — this is the scenario where I'd most want a tool that gives me permission to stop deliberating, and it did that in one clean sentence.

**5. Skeptical read.** "Name the one thing you'd want to be true before you'd feel settled. If you can't, that's your answer." — this is a good line, but it arrives right after the report already told me I'm settled, so it reads slightly contradictory: am I settled, or do I still need to pass a test? A small tension between the verdict and the closing nudge.

**6. The Pivot.** There is no Pivot, correctly — "you already know enough" is exactly the verdict my situation calls for, and I'd have said the same.

## Summary

**Three things that produced the clearest "oh" moment:**
1. Daniel's reframe — "Then you may not be deciding whether to move back home to be near my parents. You may be deciding whether you can get what you need without it." — collapsing a guilt-driven decision into a testable question.
2. Aisha's tradeoff line — "On everything you named, 'Stay' is stronger. The tradeoff isn't between the options; it's between what you're sure of and what you're not." — naming that indecision isn't always symmetrical.
3. Tom's verdict — "There's no unknown left that would materially change this decision. What's left is preference, and that's yours." — permission to stop, delivered without hedging.

**Three biggest UX/writing problems:**
1. **Broken grammar when hope is phrased as "to ___."** Current: `Can "Stay in my job and look for a raise elsewhere" actually give you to finally be doing the kind of research you actually care about instead of assisting someone else's?` The template assumes hope is a noun phrase ("less stress"), but the app's own placeholder text ("Less stress. More money. To feel like myself again…") explicitly invites "to ___" answers, which the majority of real people used here. Proposed: detect a leading "to " and strip it before insertion, or drop "actually give you" in favor of "let you ___" when the hope starts with "to": `Can "Stay in my job..." actually let you finally be doing the kind of research you care about?`
2. **`toYou()` misses possessive pronouns like "mine."** Current: `Can "Stay" actually give you to build something that's actually mine instead of making someone else rich?` Should read "...something that's actually yours...". Proposed: add `\bmine\b` → `yours` to the `toYou` regex chain in `js/engine.js`.
3. **First-person option labels leak into second-person prose.** When the question is typed in the first person with a possessive ("move back home to be near my parents," "quit my job to start my own business"), `inferOptions` keeps that phrasing as the option's label verbatim, and the label is then quoted inside second-person sentences: `You don't just want to move back home to be near my parents.` Reads as if Clarity is talking about its own parents. Proposed: run the same `toYou()` conversion the hope text gets on the inferred option label before it's ever used in report prose (keep the untouched original only for the editable input field on step 0).

**Where the engine's logic (not just wording) produced a weak Pivot:**
- `candidates()` in `js/engine.js` (line 35) fires the "hope" Pivot whenever `stillWant === "no" && hope`, unconditionally — before it ever checks whether any factor is actually uncertain. In first-pass testing of the already-knows persona (Tom) with `stillWant: "no"`, the engine produced a hope-based Pivot even though every one of his four factors was marked known — masking the "you already know enough" verdict his situation actually called for. The final persona was rewritten with `stillWant: "yes"` to correctly reach the no-Pivot branch, but the underlying rule means: anyone who is fully decided on the facts and still answers "no" to the "would you still want it" question (which is a very natural answer — "no, staying obviously can't give me retirement") will be denied the "already know enough" verdict even with zero real uncertainty. The hope-check should be gated on `uncertain(s).length` being non-zero, or at least be demoted below a genuine no-pivot verdict when nothing else is uncertain.
- For `stillWant === "unsure"`, the report explicitly tells the person "you're not sure X is the only way to get [hope]" (the `discovery()` and `reframe` text), but `candidates()` never generates a hope-based Pivot for "unsure" — only for "no" (see job-change and torn-many-unknowns, both "unsure," where the Pivot silently reverts to an ordinary factor question with no acknowledgment of the doubt the report itself just raised). This is an inconsistency between what the report says matters and what the engine actually chases: the copy raises the "is there another way to get this" question and then drops it.
- start-business shows a fidelity ceiling rather than a bug: because "runway" isn't in the fixed `factors` list and Ola never typed it as a custom factor, the engine cannot know it's the real crux — a reasonable design tradeoff (the engine only reasons about what it's told) but worth flagging since the report's confidence ("If that's true, 'Stay' has a strong case") doesn't hedge for factors the person may not have thought to name.

**Is this experience Meaningful, Different, and Salient?** Meaningful: yes, for six of eight personas it produced a genuine "oh," most convincingly wherever the "hope" mechanism worked (move-home, buy-vs-rent, grad-school) and in the clean "already know enough" case (Tom) — the emotional arc the brand promises really does land. Different: yes, clearly, from a pros-and-cons list or a friend's advice — no other format asks "would you still want it if the other option gave you the same thing" or separates known from assumed with this discipline, and the agency rule holds cleanly across all eight (never once did a report tell anyone what to choose). Salient: more uncertain — the individual sentences are memorable, but the personas mostly recalled the *idea* ("find the one unknown," "you already know enough") rather than the brand name or the ritual, and the transcripts show a mechanical seam (the "give you to ___" grammar break, the leaked first-person labels) at exactly the moment the report is supposed to feel most bespoke. The single biggest gap is that the reframe/Pivot logic is fully built for `stillWant: "no"` but only partially built for `"unsure"` — and "unsure" is arguably the most common real answer for anyone who is actually stuck (as opposed to already privately decided), so the mechanism that produces Clarity's best moments doesn't yet fire for the audience it should serve most.
